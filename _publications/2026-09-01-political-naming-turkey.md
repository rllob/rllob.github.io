Can a baby's name be a political statement? In May 1972, Turkey executed the revolutionary student leader Deniz Gezmiş, who became a martyr for the Turkish left. Deniz is an ordinary Turkish word meaning "sea," yet in the early 1970s the share of newborns named Deniz climbed sharply, stayed high through the decade, and fell back around the 1980 military coup without disappearing. This paper asks what such everyday family choices reveal about political expression under authoritarian rule, where open declarations of allegiance are costly and a name can carry political meaning while remaining perfectly legal.

Answering that question means telling a political trend apart from a passing fashion, and national events like these leave no untouched region to compare against. I build the comparison from the civil birth register itself: more than 28 million births from 1960 to 1990, and forty secular names brought into use by the same 1930s language reform as Deniz and matched to its earlier popularity. Among them, Deniz stands out. Its combined rise and fall ranks first, and it contracted most where it had spread most and where the Turkish Workers' Party had won more votes in 1969, a pattern consistent with a name that carried a political signal and became costlier to choose under repression. The records cannot reveal what individual parents intended, but the design shows how administrative records of ordinary choices can serve as evidence of political expression when no unaffected comparison group exists.
