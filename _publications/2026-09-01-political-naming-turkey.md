Can a newborn's name reveal political expression under repression? Deniz, the Turkish word for "sea," acquired a political association through the persecution and execution of the revolutionary Deniz Gezmiş. Using more than 28 million domestic birth records from 1960 to 1990, I examine the name's rise during the 1970s and contraction around the 1980 military coup. The challenge is to distinguish political expression from changing naming fashions when national political events leave no geographically unexposed comparison group.

The methodological contribution combines two comparisons with a model of geographic variation. Panel interrupted time series estimate departures from a specified continuation of Deniz's earlier trajectory, while permutation comparisons with forty names matched on their pre-1971 histories assess whether its response is unusual among names with similar cultural origins. Bayesian multilevel models address a separate measurement question by jointly estimating local diffusion and suppression responses, their correlation, and their spatial structure. These latent responses measure where naming expanded and contracted, with uncertainty, rather than treating noisy local estimates as directly observed quantities.

Deniz ranks first on the joint growth-and-contraction statistic among the forty-one names. Larger modeled contractions occur where the earlier increase was greater and prior support for the Turkish Workers' Party was stronger. Together, these patterns support a political interpretation, conditional on the trend projections, comparability of the selected names, and registry coverage. The records cannot establish individual parents' intentions or isolate the effects of particular events within each political episode.
